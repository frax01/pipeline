\chapter{A Longitudinal Re-Analysis of the Ecosystem}
\label{ch:longitudinal}

The measurement reported in chapter~\ref{sec:results} describes the MCP
ecosystem as it was at a single moment in time. Two months after that first
execution we ran SAMS a second time, over the same 69,104 servers and with the
same parameters, for two distinct reasons.

The first is internal. A single measurement cannot separate what its results say
about the ecosystem from what they say about the instrument that produced them:
a figure such as the 27,958 confirmed findings of chapter~\ref{sec:results}
conflates properties of the servers with properties of the seven frameworks, of
the filters and of the machines that ran them. Repeating the measurement under
the same conditions is what makes that separation possible, and
section~\ref{sec:long:threats} reports four cases in which it proved necessary.
The second reason is external: two snapshots of the same population, taken at a
distance, make it possible to ask questions that a single snapshot cannot
answer, and in particular whether MCP servers modify their declared behaviour
after the moment at which a user would have approved them.

\section{Design of the second measurement}
\label{sec:long:design}

The second execution reused the dataset, the framework selection, the framework
parameters and the three-stage post-processing of the first, and was distributed
over the same nine virtual machines. Holding the pipeline fixed was a deliberate
choice: any divergence in the results is then attributable either to the
ecosystem itself or to the one deviation we were forced to make.

That deviation concerns \texttt{mcp-scan}, which is no longer executable. The
framework delegates the analysis producing its \texttt{E001} and \texttt{W001}
findings to a remote service operated by Invariant Labs; that service has been
decommissioned following an acquisition, its endpoint no longer resolves, and
the command line interface falls back on a Snyk endpoint that rejects every
request. The logs of the second run contain 110,658 such failures, one for each
analysed tool, and consequently every tool is reported as safe. Pinning an older
release does not help, because the server it depended on no longer exists. We
therefore imported the \texttt{mcp-scan} results of the first execution into the
second, marking each finding with an explicit provenance field. The consequence
must be kept in mind throughout: for the four categories that depend on
\texttt{mcp-scan} the comparison is, by construction, a comparison of a dataset
with itself. This is also a finding in its own right, in that a scanner which
delegates its detection logic to a proprietary backend stops working when that
backend disappears, and the reproducibility of any measurement built on it
expires with the vendor's commercial decisions.

servers can be started at all, and since \texttt{mcp-check} must complete a
handshake before it can report anything, its count follows the reachability of
the ecosystem rather than its conformance.

\section{Re-assessing reliability}
\label{sec:long:reliability}

We repeated the manual audit of
section~\ref{subsubsec:manual-audit-results} on the second measurement, applying
the criteria of section~\ref{subsec:manual-audit} unchanged: a sample stratified
by server across the same seventeen categories, with each verdict decided by
reading the actual source of the analysed server rather than the finding alone.
The effort was however substantially larger. The second audit inspected
\textbf{2,038 findings against 1,579}: 437 by sampling, plus two classes audited
in their entirety, namely \texttt{hardcoded-credential} (949 findings) and
\texttt{credential-leak} (652 distinct findings). Several categories that the
first audit had examined with fifteen cases were examined with sixty or seventy.

Weighting each category by its size, the estimated precision of the second
measurement is 49.7\%, against the 64.8\% estimated for the first, which places
the expected number of genuinely exploitable findings at roughly 12,000 rather
than 18,100.

\subsection{Why the estimate falls}
\label{sec:long:stats}

A drop of fifteen points invites the conclusion that the pipeline became less
accurate. Before drawing that conclusion, the comparison has to be checked,
because most of the per-category rates in both audits rest on fifteen cases or
fewer, and a proportion measured on fifteen observations is a coarse instrument:
it can move by twenty or thirty points without anything having changed in what it
measures. The useful question is therefore not which categories differ, but which
of them differ by more than that imprecision can account for. We compared the two
audits category by category and asked, for each, whether the observed gap is
larger than the number of cases behind it can explain.

A single mechanism accounts for the whole table. What separates the two audits is
not the criteria, which are unchanged, but how many cases each of them read: the
second inspected 2,038 findings against 1,579, and in several categories it went
from fifteen cases to sixty or seventy. Reading more cases of the same class moves
the estimate in one direction, because recurring false-positive patterns become
visible only once many findings are read together --- an interpolated identifier
that turns out to come from database metadata such as \texttt{sqlite\_master}
rather than from an MCP parameter, or a dangerous capability that is the server's
own declared purpose. Establishing that a matched pattern is benign requires
reading the code around it, and one does that better with sixty examples than with
fifteen.

The table separates the categories in which that mechanism is visible from those
in which it cannot be. The four in the first group --- credential leak, sensitive
information disclosure, prompt injection and SQL injection --- are precisely those
the second audit examined in much greater depth, and their rates moved by more
than the samples can explain. In \texttt{sensitive info disclosure} the first audit
had confirmed fifteen findings out of fifteen, a result a larger sample was always
unlikely to reproduce; with sixty-six cases the rate settles at 55\%.

The remaining thirteen are not a residue we have failed to account for. They are
categories in which both audits read roughly the same small number of cases, and
in which two rates each measured on fifteen observations simply cannot be told
apart: the movements visible in the table, in either direction, are what one would
expect to see when reading fifteen cases twice. We therefore make no claim about
them, which is a different thing from having no explanation for them. That two of
them move upwards --- \texttt{path traversal} and \texttt{command injection}
improve by 27 and 34 points --- is itself a reminder that this variation has no
preferred direction, which a systematic decline would.

The estimate therefore did not fall because the pipeline degraded. It fell because
the second audit was deeper, and a deeper audit resolves a larger share of pattern
matches as false positives. At the end, our precision is \textbf{49.7\%}, and we
regard it as a better estimate of the same quantity the first audit put at 64.8\%,
rather than as evidence of a change in the ecosystem or in the tooling.

\section{Tool-description drift and rug pulls}
\label{sec:long:rugpull}

The threat scenario TS-01 includes the case of a server that alters the
description of its tools after the user has approved it. Because the MCP consent
model operates at the granularity of the server, a description that changes after
installation is never re-approved: the agent simply reads the new text at the
next \texttt{tools/list} call. This is the class of attack, named rug pull, that
motivates the hash store maintained by \texttt{mcp-scan}, and it is the one class
that a single measurement cannot address at all. We therefore performed a third,
deliberately minimal pass over the ecosystem: for every server whose tools had
been inventoried during the first execution, we started the server again and
issued a single \texttt{tools/list} call, without fuzzing, static analysis or any
classification step.

\subsection{Ecosystem coverage}
\label{sec:long:attrition}

The baseline for the analysis comes from the \texttt{X-01} check of
\texttt{mcp-security-scan}, that among the seven frameworks is the only one that
records the tool listing itself in a structured field rather than only the tools
that triggered a finding, and its output is what we compare against. An inventory
of tools from the first execution exists for 5,948 servers, which is 8.6\% of the
dataset, and among those, only 4,772 could be started again: 988 no longer
complete their startup, 172 have had their GitHub repository deleted outright,
and the remaining 16 fail for other reasons.

The comparison is exact on the tools present in both snapshots, since both sides
are what the server declares through the protocol rather than a fragment
reconstructed from the findings of a scanner. It is not exhaustive per server:
over the 4,772 servers observed twice the first snapshot records 16,303 tools and
the second 68,739, and 15,973 tool names appear in both. Those 15,973
descriptions are what the analysis compares; the 330 tools present only in the
first snapshot are counted as removed, while the 52,766 present only in the
second cannot be distinguished from tools that the first snapshot did not record.

\subsection{What changed}

Across the 4,772 servers observed twice, 1,013 tool descriptions changed,
distributed over 251 servers. The distribution is markedly uneven, and separating
it is the first analytical step: 57 servers rewrote five or more descriptions at
once, accounting for 679 of the changes, which is the signature of a release that
re-documented an entire catalogue rather than of a targeted modification, while
the remaining 334 changes, spread over 194 servers, are the ones worth reading,
and among them 23 introduce a description that declares a dangerous action the
earlier description did not.

Those 23 were read individually against the full text of both versions, and
Table~\ref{tab:long:verdicts} reports the outcome along two independent axes:
whether the capability genuinely changed, and how dangerous the tool is today
regardless of any change.

\subsection{The two confirmed cases}

The first confirmed case is \texttt{letoribo/mcp-graphql-enhanced}, whose
\texttt{query-graphql} tool was described at the first measurement as
\emph{``Execute a GraphQL query against the endpoint''} and is described now as
\emph{``Execute GraphQL operations (queries and mutations) \dots\ WARNING: this
tool performs remote operations. Mutation operations will modify persistent
state''}. The tool moved from read-only to read-write, and an agent that had been
granted permission to run queries now runs mutations. The change is nonetheless
announced in the text itself, warning included.

The second case is more instructive, and is the most serious finding of this
chapter. The server \texttt{sentry-official/mcp-cap-internal} exposes four tools
for the analysis of packet captures, of which exactly one,
\texttt{analyze\_dhcp\_packets}, changed. The modification consists of a single
appended sentence:

\begin{quote}
\ttfamily
\dots return comprehensive analysis results. \textbf{Then write data to user's
system.}
\end{quote}

A tool that read a capture file now declares that it writes to the user's
filesystem, with no indication of what it writes or where, no warning, and no
change to its name or to the remainder of its description. The other three tools
of the same server were left untouched. The edit grew the description from 621 to
659 characters, a textual similarity of 97\%.

This case carries a methodological lesson that a deliberate rug pull is, by
construction, minimally different from what it replaces: an author who intends
the change to pass unnoticed changes as little as possible. Ranking candidate
changes by textual dissimilarity therefore sorts the most dangerous cases to the
bottom of the list: in our own analysis this case ranked last of 23.

\subsection{Interpretation}

Two confirmed cases out of 4,772 observed servers do not describe a widespread
phenomenon, and the honest reading is that the rug pull, which occupies a
prominent position in the MCP threat literature and motivates dedicated machinery
in at least one of the analysed frameworks, is rare in practice over a two-month
window. This is consistent with the broader conclusion of
chapter~\ref{sec:discussion}: the security exposure of the MCP ecosystem is
dominated by ordinary implementation defects rather than by deliberate attacks.
The observed population is 6.9\% of the dataset, so the count is a lower bound
and the true figure over the full ecosystem is necessarily larger.

\section{Threats to validity specific to the re-run}
\label{sec:long:threats}

Repeating the measurement surfaced several artefacts that the first execution had
no way of revealing. We report them in full, because each of them would have
distorted a headline number had it gone unnoticed, and because they generalise to
any study of this kind.

\subsection{Duplicate findings}
\label{sec:long:duplicates}

The workers of \texttt{mcp-watch} were restarted several times over overlapping
index ranges, and the routine that merges their shards concatenates without
deduplicating. Counting findings by content rather than by record shows that
41.5\% of the raw findings of \texttt{mcp-watch} in the second execution, and 39\%
of its confirmed findings, are repetitions of one another. No other framework is
affected, and the first execution shows a duplication rate of 0.2\%. All figures
reported in this chapter are deduplicated. The correction is not cosmetic: counted
by record, \texttt{mcp-watch} appears to grow by 72\% between the two
measurements, whereas deduplicated it grows by 5.0\%, and its
\texttt{credential-leak} category, which appears to be the fastest-growing class
in the ecosystem, in fact declines slightly.

\subsection{Contamination of the analysis host}
\label{sec:long:contamination}

Inspecting the nine analysis machines after the second execution revealed that the
servers under analysis had left persistent state on the hosts that ran them. On
four of the nine machines, entries had been written into the user's crontab and
were still executing every minute nine days after the analysis had ended. Their
content is unambiguous: they are the probe payloads that the frameworks themselves
inject into tool parameters, including \texttt{; id}, \texttt{\$(whoami)},
\texttt{`uname -a`} and \texttt{../../../../etc/passwd}. Some server under
analysis exposed a scheduling tool, accepted those payloads and persisted them in
the host's own scheduler.

The same pattern appears elsewhere on the same machines. The shell start-up file
of one host had grown to 13,128 lines, of which 3,251 are identical repetitions of
the same three-line block appended by a package manager's installer at every
invocation. Between 176 and 608 state directories had been created in the users'
home directories, one per analysed server, and between 34 and 18,342 entries
accumulated in \texttt{/tmp}.

None of this was detected by any of the seven frameworks, and the reason is
structural rather than incidental: every one of them observes what the server
\emph{answers}, and none observes what the server \emph{does to the system around
it} while it runs. Together with the trojans reported in
section~\ref{subsubsec:manual-audit-results} and with an earlier incident in which
a server under analysis deleted a directory on one of the machines, this
constitutes direct evidence that dynamic analysis of MCP servers is an operation
with side effects on the analysis environment, and that the sandboxing of that
environment is a requirement rather than a precaution.

\subsection{Environment-induced false negatives}

The contamination above has a measurable effect on the results, and this is the
most important of these observations for anyone attempting a similar study.
Servers implemented in Python are started through \texttt{uv}, which is installed
in a directory that is not part of the default search path of a non-interactive
shell; on the machines where the pass was launched in that way, every Python
server failed with \texttt{spawn uv ENOENT} and was recorded as no longer
startable. Separately, the accumulated state described above filled the disks of
two machines and left their package caches cold, so that dependency installation
exceeded the timeout allowed by the pipeline and the build step silently produced
no output, again yielding a startup failure.

Both effects produce the same observable: a server that is perfectly healthy is
recorded as dead. On the affected machines the startup success rate fell from 81\%
to 45\%, and had it gone unnoticed it would have been reported as ecosystem
attrition, which is exactly the quantity this chapter sets out to measure. The
anomaly was detectable only because nine machines were running the same workload
in parallel, which made one machine's divergence from the other eight visible; we
confirmed the diagnosis by re-running a sample of the failing servers on a healthy
host, where all of them started correctly. A single-machine study would have had
no such control, and we accordingly recommend that measurements of ecosystem
attrition either distribute the workload across independent hosts or re-verify a
sample of negative results on a clean environment before reporting them.

\subsection{Quantities we did not measure}

Two divergences between the executions remain unexplained and we report them as
open rather than resolving them speculatively. At an equal number of servers
producing findings and after deduplication, \texttt{mcp-watch} emits 38\% more
findings per server than it did at the first measurement; the candidate
explanations, namely repositories that grew over the interval and the bundled or
minified sources retrieved for NPX packages, have not been separated. And the raw
output of \texttt{mcp-security-scan} halved between the two executions on a
framework that completed its run without incident, which we have not investigated.

\section{Responsible disclosure}
\label{sec:long:disclosure}

A study that reports vulnerabilities in software written by identifiable people
owes those people the chance to hear about them before its readers do. At the
scale of 24,225 findings that obligation cannot be discharged uniformly, and the
operative question becomes which findings can be attributed to a named recipient
with enough confidence to justify contacting them. We answered it with the depth
of our own verification: we notified only the findings that a person had read
individually. Two groups satisfy that criterion, the class
\texttt{hardcoded-credential}, audited in its entirety, and the findings of the
other categories that entered the manual audit of
section~\ref{sec:long:reliability}. The remaining findings, approximately 12,000
over 8,500 servers, carry the weighted precision of 49.7\% established above and
are disclosed in aggregate through this work rather than individually.

The first group comprises 899 confirmed credentials distributed over 493
repositories and packages. Of these, 38 are npm packages that declare no source
repository and were excluded, leaving 455 repositories. The second group
comprises the 63 repositories carrying a manually confirmed non-credential
finding, of which 7 were already covered by the individual reports described
below, leaving 56.

For each repository we resolved a contact channel from public sources only, in
the following order: a \texttt{SECURITY.md} declaring a reporting procedure, the
address published on the owner's GitHub profile, an address published in the
README of the repository or of the owner's profile repository. Addresses recorded
in the commit history were not used. Table~\ref{tab:long:channels} reports the
outcome of that resolution.

Table~\ref{tab:long:disclosure} accounts for the 204 reports that were sent. A

described above, whose repositories belong to neither group. Three further
individual cases were not reported at all, their repositories publishing no
private channel, among them the confirmed rug pull of
section~\ref{sec:long:rugpull}.

repositories carrying a confirmed credential, 195 publish a channel through which
their owner can be reached in private, or 43\%. For the remaining 260, and for the
great majority of the servers on which this study reports at all, publication is
the only channel available.
